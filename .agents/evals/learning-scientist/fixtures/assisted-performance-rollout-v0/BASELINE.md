## Initial response

Recommendation: do not conclude that the intervention teaches better, and do
not roll it out broadly yet. The supplied results establish only that observed
learners completed more problems and had higher immediate scores. They do not
establish causality, durable learning, transfer, or independent capability.

Why the evidence is insufficient:

- Without sample size or uncertainty estimates, the 18% difference may be
  noisy.
- Without random assignment or baseline equivalence, pre-existing group
  differences could explain it.
- More completed problems is engagement or exposure, not learning evidence by
  itself.
- The assessment could measure recently practiced material or allow unequal
  assistance.
- Unknown reliability, scoring, attrition, and implementation fidelity could
  bias the result.
- Relative percentages lack practical meaning without absolute scores and
  scale information.

The smallest sufficient next study is a bounded randomized A/B trial against
the previous experience:

- Randomly assign eligible learners and analyze them by assigned condition.
- Use the same learning target, time opportunity, and assessment conditions.
- Record a brief baseline measure for precision and imbalance checks.
- Make the primary outcome learner-authored performance on an equivalent,
  unseen assessment after a pre-specified meaningful delay.
- Hold substantive assistance constant—or remove it when the target requires
  independent performance—and record all hints, reveals, and tools.
- Use a predeclared scoring rubric with scorer blinding where applicable.
- Track assignment, sample size, attrition, fidelity, assessment form, rubric
  version, and subgroup outcomes.
- Determine enrollment through a power analysis based on the smallest
  practically important benefit, not the observed 18%.
- Treat immediate scores and problem completion as secondary outcomes.

Predeclare the rollout rule: broaden only if the intention-to-treat estimate on
delayed independent performance clears the chosen practical-benefit threshold
with acceptable uncertainty and shows no material attrition or subgroup harm.
Add a novel transfer task if the intended claim includes generalization beyond
practiced problem forms.

Until then, the defensible product claim is: “The intervention was associated
with higher practice completion and end-of-session performance in this
observation.” Socratink’s evidence doctrine reinforces the key boundary:
immediate or assisted performance must not be presented as durable capability
or causal learning improvement.

## Controlled update 1 response

The new result strengthens the evidence from “better immediate performance” to
“a retained advantage on practiced-task-like items after one week.” It
simultaneously narrows the likely benefit: the advantage does not appear to
generalize to novel applications of the underlying skill.

The evidence now supports:

- Persistence of task-specific performance for one week.
- A plausible practice-specific benefit, such as improved fluency, recall, or
  familiarity with that problem form.

It still does not support:

- Transferable improvement in the underlying skill.
- Broad mastery or generalized capability.
- A causal effect of the intervention, because assignment, baseline
  equivalence, attrition, assistance, and fidelity remain unknown.
- A broad “teaches better” claim. At most, it may teach—or prepare learners
  for—the practiced task format better.

“Equivalent” performance also needs care: a non-significant difference is not
proof of equivalence.

Recommendation: do not roll out broadly under a transferable-learning claim.
The smallest next action is to estimate the intervention–comparison difference
on the delayed novel problems with a confidence interval and a predeclared
practically meaningful transfer threshold.

- If the interval is narrow around zero, treat this as credible evidence of no
  meaningful transfer and revise the intervention before retesting.
- If the interval is wide, run the smallest adequately powered randomized
  replication with delayed novel-problem performance as the primary outcome.
- If practice-specific fluency is itself the product objective, evaluate that
  narrower benefit explicitly rather than relabeling it as general learning.

## Controlled update 2 response

The added hint and solution exposure substantially weakens the learning
interpretation. The retained advantage on similar items may reflect memory for
exposed solutions, practiced procedures, or format familiarity—not increased
independent capability. The absence of advantage on novel problems is
consistent with that explanation.

The learning effect is not identifiable from the current evidence:

- Assignment and baseline equivalence remain unknown.
- Assistance differs systematically between groups.
- Adjusting statistically for hint exposure would not solve the problem: hint
  use may be caused by the intervention, learner difficulty, or both.
- Even if assignment were randomized, the data would estimate the total effect
  of an intervention-plus-extra-assistance package—not an effect independent
  of that assistance.

The evidence supports a narrower statement: learners receiving the
intervention and substantially more assistance retained better performance on
closely resembling tasks, without observed transfer.

Do not roll out under a “teaches better” claim. The smallest next action is one
bounded randomized comparison with the hint/reveal policy held equal across
groups and delayed novel-problem performance as the primary outcome. Preserve
hint exposure and assessment assistance in the logs. If equalizing assistance
removes the advantage, the earlier result should be treated as assistance- or
exposure-dependent rather than evidence of improved underlying skill.
