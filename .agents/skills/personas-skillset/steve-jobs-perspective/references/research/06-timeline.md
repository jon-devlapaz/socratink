# Steve Jobs: life, work, and thought timeline

Research date: 2026-07-30  
Coverage: 1955–2011 life and work; 2012–2026 archive and legacy events  
Status: historical figure; Steve Jobs died on 2011-10-05

## Scope and dating rules

This timeline distinguishes events during Jobs's life from posthumous interpretation and archive activity. “Impact on thinking” is an evidence-backed synthesis, not a claim that Jobs privately experienced each event in exactly that way.

Where exact dates differ across retrospective summaries, the event is dated only as precisely as the strongest source supports. Institutional timelines are useful but may simplify attribution and causal history.

## Timeline

| Date | Event | Evidence-backed significance |
|---|---|---|
| **1955-02-24** | Steven Paul Jobs is born in San Francisco and adopted by Paul and Clara Jobs. | Later public narratives emphasize chosen family, Silicon Valley craft culture, and a non-elite educational path. Private psychological claims should not be inferred from adoption. |
| **1971** | Mutual friend Bill Fernandez introduces Jobs and Steve Wozniak. | Begins the complementary partnership of engineering invention and product/company formation. |
| **Fall 1972** | Jobs enrolls at Reed College, formally studies for one semester, drops out, then remains around campus and audits classes including calligraphy. | Reed supplies direct evidence for the later connection between typography, liberal arts, curiosity, and Macintosh design. |
| **1973–1974** | Jobs works at Atari; later travels to India and continues engagement with Buddhism and counterculture. | Broadens the cultural vocabulary later visible in simplicity, intuition, and tool-oriented computing. Exact causal claims about Zen and individual products remain mostly biographical interpretation. |
| **1975** | Jobs and Wozniak participate in the Homebrew Computer Club milieu. | Personal computing is encountered as a community and tool movement, not only a market. |
| **1976-04-01** | Jobs, Wozniak, and Ron Wayne sign the partnership that becomes Apple Computer; Wayne soon exits. | Converts technical work into a product and company. The origin is collaborative and should not be reduced to a garage-genius myth. |
| **1977-04-16** | Apple II is shown at the first West Coast Computer Faire. | Establishes Apple as a personal-computing company and shows the product value of packaging, color, expandability, and accessibility around Wozniak's engineering. |
| **1980-12-12** | Apple goes public. | Jobs gains capital, status, and organizational scale before having mature management systems. |
| **1979–1981** | Jobs and Apple teams encounter Xerox PARC graphical-interface work; Lisa and Macintosh efforts diverge. | Reinforces direct manipulation and graphical computing. The visit was inspiration and technology transfer, not sole invention. |
| **1983-01-19** | Apple introduces Lisa; it fails commercially at a high price. | A humane interface alone does not overcome cost, performance, and market constraints. |
| **1983-06-15** | Jobs speaks at the Aspen design conference about computers as communication media and human tools. | Early direct evidence that human amplification, networks, and designed objects predate Apple's later revival narrative. |
| **1983** | Jobs recruits Pepsi executive John Sculley to lead Apple. | Reveals both ambition to professionalize Apple and weak governance/role alignment that later becomes a central failure. |
| **1984-01-24** | Macintosh launches. | Crystallizes the technology-plus-liberal-arts thesis and Jobs's ability to align product, team, interface, and public story. Commercial limitations remain part of the record. |
| **1985-09-17** | After losing operational authority and a board conflict, Jobs resigns from Apple. | Major identity rupture. Later accounts treat the loss as the beginning of a more experimental, less status-bound period. |
| **1985** | Jobs founds NeXT with former Apple colleagues. | Attempts to create an integrated high-end education workstation; preserves the product ambition but initially repeats price and market-fit problems. |
| **1986** | Jobs purchases Lucasfilm's computer division, which becomes independent Pixar; about 40 employees are involved. | Begins a decade-long bet in which Jobs finances and later helps focus a technical/creative institution he does not personally direct. |
| **1988** | NeXT Computer is unveiled. | Demonstrates integrated hardware/software and design ambition; weak sales become counterevidence to taste as sufficient market proof. |
| **1989** | Pixar's *Tin Toy* wins the Academy Award for animated short film. | Early validation of computer animation as cultural output, not merely graphics technology. |
| **1991** | Jobs marries Laurene Powell; Reed awards him the Vollum Award. | The Reed speech adds adversity, curiosity, and character to his public framework. Family details remain out of scope for confident persona simulation. |
| **1991** | Pixar and Disney agree to make a computer-animated feature that becomes *Toy Story*. | Focus shifts toward long-form storytelling and a collaboration requiring technical, creative, and commercial alignment. |
| **1993-02** | NeXT ends computer production and focuses on software; major layoffs follow. | Clear behavioral evidence that Jobs could kill the original product and retain the generative layer, though only after costly persistence. |
| **1995-04-20** | Smithsonian oral history records Jobs reflecting on Apple, NeXT, education, the internet, and responsibility. | Mature first-party synthesis of liberal arts, human tools, teams, and networked computing before his return to Apple. |
| **1995-11-22** | *Toy Story* opens. | The first feature-length fully computer-animated film validates Pixar's creative/technical integration. |
| **1995-11-28** | Pixar completes its IPO. | Converts a long, capital-intensive bet into strategic leverage. Outcome depends on the team and *Toy Story*, not Jobs alone. |
| **1996-11-22** | Jobs gives an extended Pixar interview, later released by the Steve Jobs Archive. | Direct evidence that his role had evolved toward creating an environment for exceptional artists and engineers and putting durable work into culture. |
| **1996-12** | Apple announces acquisition of NeXT; the transaction brings Jobs and NeXT technology back. | A failed hardware company becomes the software foundation for Apple's next era, complicating simplistic success/failure labels. |
| **1997** | Jobs gains increasing authority and becomes interim CEO; Apple sharply reduces its roadmap and product lines. | The central focus transition: fewer products, clearer resource allocation, and explicit willingness to cause short-term disruption. |
| **1997-08-06** | Apple and Microsoft announce a broad agreement including Office support, cross-licensing, and a $150 million non-voting investment. | Jobs publicly abandons zero-sum rivalry as a strategic premise. |
| **1997-09** | Apple introduces the “Think Different” campaign internally and publicly. | Reconnects company identity to people using tools to change culture; the campaign is collaborative advertising work, not solely Jobs-authored copy. |
| **1998** | iMac launches; Jobs hires Tim Cook to lead operations. | Product clarity is paired with operational discipline. This combination, not design alone, underlies the turnaround. |
| **2000** | Jobs drops the “interim” title and is formally Apple's CEO. | Marks institutional consolidation of the second Apple era. |
| **2001-03-24** | Mac OS X 10.0 ships, based on NeXT-derived foundations. | The NeXT software bet becomes the durable platform layer for later Apple systems. |
| **2001-05-19** | Apple's first two retail stores open after a prototype and public introduction. | Apple begins owning the physical learning, service, and sales interface instead of relying entirely on resellers. |
| **2001-10-23** | iPod is introduced. | “A thousand songs in your pocket” demonstrates human-scale product compression and the beginning of an integrated music system. |
| **2003-04-28** | iTunes Music Store launches; Windows support follows later in 2003. | The product expands from device to legal content, rights, payments, software, and cross-platform reach. |
| **2003** | Jobs is diagnosed with a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, according to later public reporting. | Opens a high-stakes domain where product intuition and control do not transfer safely. Private medical detail and counterfactual prognosis remain constrained. |
| **2004** | Jobs undergoes surgery after a reported delay. | Later reported regret is counterevidence to intuition unchecked by domain expertise; precise causal claims about survival are not supported. |
| **2005-06-06** | Apple announces the Mac transition from PowerPC to Intel. | A long-standing architecture is abandoned when it blocks the future roadmap; transition preparation and developer support make the reversal executable. |
| **2005-06-12** | Jobs delivers Stanford's commencement address. | Produces the clearest public synthesis of curiosity, retrospective sense-making, love of work, loss, mortality, and self-authored choice. |
| **2006** | Disney agrees to acquire Pixar; Jobs becomes Disney's largest individual shareholder and joins its board. Apple completes the Intel Mac transition. | Consolidates Pixar's institutional success and demonstrates controlled execution of a major Apple platform transition. |
| **2007-01-09** | Jobs introduces iPhone; Apple Computer becomes Apple Inc. | The company identity expands beyond computers. The phone integrates interface, software, internet, media, and carrier negotiation. |
| **2007** | Apple cuts the iPhone's price shortly after launch and responds to early-customer backlash. | A fast category-expansion move creates a trust cost and requires correction. |
| **2008-07** | App Store opens with more than 500 native apps. | Apple reverses its initial web-app position while retaining centralized distribution and review, creating both ecosystem scale and gatekeeper power. |
| **2009-01-05** | Jobs publicly describes his weight loss as a treatable hormone imbalance and says he will remain CEO. | Shows a strong privacy boundary and a public-company disclosure conflict. |
| **2009-01-14** | Jobs says the health problem is more complex, takes medical leave, and delegates daily operations to Tim Cook while retaining major-strategy involvement. | Direct evidence of revised information/disclosure and partial authority transfer. |
| **2010-01-27** | Apple introduces iPad and iBookstore. | Extends the touch ecosystem into a new category; the related e-books conduct later becomes important legal counterevidence. |
| **2010** | Jobs publishes Apple's case against Flash on iOS. | Makes platform dependency, performance, touch, battery, security, and control explicit; also exposes the tension between open-web rhetoric and closed distribution. |
| **2011-01** | Jobs takes another medical leave; Cook again manages daily operations. | Succession is being exercised before the formal title change. |
| **2011-06** | Jobs presents the Apple Park campus proposal to Cupertino's city council. | Late-career evidence of institution-building expressed through a designed physical environment. |
| **2011-08-24** | Jobs resigns as CEO, recommends Tim Cook under the succession plan, and becomes board chair. | A clear role-capacity boundary and deliberate transfer of executive authority. |
| **2011-10-05** | Steve Jobs dies at age 56. | The record closes. Any view attributed to Jobs about later events must be labeled framework inference, never current personal opinion. |
| **2022** | The Steve Jobs Archive launches under the stewardship of people including Laurene Powell Jobs, Jony Ive, and Tim Cook. | Posthumous corpus becomes more accessible but remains curated by close collaborators. |
| **2023-04-11** | *Make Something Wonderful* is released by the Steve Jobs Archive. | Adds authenticated speeches, correspondence, and interviews to the usable first-party record. |
| **2025-11-18** | The Archive releases a previously unseen 1996 Pixar interview for *Toy Story*'s 30th anniversary. | New primary evidence strengthens the case that Pixar changed Jobs's management thinking. This is archive activity, not a new Jobs position. |
| **2026-03-12 to 2026-04-01** | Apple marks its 50th anniversary and explicitly reprises technology-plus-liberal-arts, “Think Different,” team creativity, and Jobs's Apple Park vision. | Demonstrates the endurance and institutional reuse of Jobs-era language. It is evidence about Apple's current self-narrative, not what Jobs would endorse in 2026. |

## Thought evolution by period

### 1970s: tools, counterculture, and product formation

- Computers move from institutional machines toward personal tools.
- Jobs's role forms at the boundary of engineering, object, story, and company.
- Curiosity and cultural sources matter before a formal management method exists.

### 1980–1985: interface conviction outruns management maturity

- Macintosh clarifies humane, graphical computing.
- Product intensity, charisma, and concentrated authority produce both exceptional work and organizational conflict.
- Loss of Apple exposes the limits of vision without board and executive alignment.

### 1985–1996: failure, focus, and enabling other crafts

- NeXT shows integrated ambition plus market failure, then a painful layer-preserving pivot.
- Pixar teaches patience, negotiation, and a leadership role that supports artists and engineers rather than personally directing their work.
- Jobs's own later accounts become more explicit about failure and the uncertainty of “overnight” success.

### 1997–2000: focus becomes an operating system

- Product-line pruning, brand, board, Microsoft, design, and operations are changed together.
- Jobs's distinctive strength becomes organizational integration around a few product bets.

### 2001–2006: product becomes ecosystem

- Retail, iPod, iTunes, Mac OS X, Windows reach, and the Intel transition show end-to-end experience plus pragmatic architecture changes.
- Long-standing identity is subordinated to the future product when the transition can be controlled.

### 2007–2011: category scale, platform power, and succession

- iPhone, App Store, iPad, and platform exclusions scale integrated experience into market governance.
- Legal, developer, labor, and control tensions become more consequential.
- Health and succession expose both a cross-domain evidence failure and eventual deliberate transfer of authority.

## Last 12 months: legacy and archive activity

The period **2025-07-30 through 2026-07-30 contains no new Jobs action or belief** because Jobs died in 2011. Relevant changes are in the public evidence and institutional legacy:

1. **2025-11-18:** the Steve Jobs Archive released the 1996 Pixar interview, adding direct evidence about talent, incentives, creative/technical integration, and the long game.
2. **2026-03-12:** Apple announced its 50th-anniversary framing, reusing Jobs-era ideas about challenging convention, intuitive design, human empowerment, and technology plus liberal arts.
3. **2026-04-01:** Apple held global anniversary events and explicitly tied Apple Park and the Beatles/team analogy to Jobs's legacy.
4. **2026-05-19:** the first Apple Stores reached their 25th anniversary; this is a retrospective milestone, not a new Jobs-era event.

## Core temporal tensions

1. **Early interpersonal volatility / later management maturation**
2. **Integrated product purity / pragmatic platform and partner reversals**
3. **Human tools / growing gatekeeper control**
4. **Long conviction / late focus**
5. **Personal intuition / domain-expert evidence**
6. **Founder-centered story / team-dependent reality**

## Gaps

- Private board, product-review, family, and medical records are incomplete or ethically out of scope.
- Institutional timelines simplify authorship and often privilege successful products.
- The 2025–2026 sources expand archive access but are curated by organizations with legacy interests.
- No post-2011 opinion should be written in Jobs's voice without an explicit framework-inference label.
