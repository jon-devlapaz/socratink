# Sal Khan — External Views, Evaluations, and Criticism

Research completed 2026-08-08. Scope: independent scholarly/government evaluations, identifiable education commentators, and reviews of *The One World Schoolhouse*. This report does **not** treat platform marketing, popularity, or anecdotes as proof of effectiveness.

## Executive reading

The most defensible external characterization is neither “technology replaces teaching” nor “Khan Academy is merely videos.” Khan’s unusually strong contribution is the free, reusable combination of explanations, practice, feedback, and teacher-facing progress data. Independent and public evaluations find value especially when those pieces are deliberately embedded in teacher-led instruction. The empirical record is heterogeneous: a short controlled study found no significant score effect; a large recent quasi-experimental PNAS study reports small positive average math effects, with higher achievers benefiting more; an IES trial of a broader summer program reported large algebra-readiness gains but cannot isolate the Khan Academy hour from the other three daily hours of instruction.

Khan’s core mastery/pace vision attracts support as a humane corrective to moving students onward with unresolved prerequisites. The recurring criticism is that a polished, asynchronous explanation cannot by itself diagnose misconceptions, motivate practice, supply relationships, or make a conventional lecture into inquiry-driven learning. That is a design constraint and implementation warning, not evidence that the platform has no value.

## Evidence and external assessments

### 1. Accessible mastery support is a genuine strength

- **[External finding | high reliability]** Francis Schrag (University of Wisconsin–Madison, emeritus), in a scholarly essay review, calls Khan arguably the most successful and famous figure to turn web-enabled education ideas into an actual institution. He identifies a concrete equity benefit: a mathematically talented learner with internet access but weak local instruction can repeatedly access a capable explanation and practice; parents and homeschoolers can similarly use an always-available, no-cost resource. Importantly, Schrag explicitly adds: “Not every child will be able to learn in this way.” [Education Review PDF](https://edrev.asu.edu/index.php/ER/article/download/1431/101/212)

- **[External finding | medium reliability]** Kirkus judged *The One World Schoolhouse* a “fresh, welcome approach,” accurately summarizing its appeal: self-paced, mastery-oriented video learning supplemented by peers and teachers in class. This is a professional trade review, useful for how the vision landed publicly but not efficacy evidence. [Kirkus review](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/salman-khan/one-world-schoolhouse/)

- **[External finding | high reliability]** A U.S. Institute of Education Sciences (IES)-funded cluster RCT in 20 California community colleges (588 students, 34 instructors) found that including Khan Academy in blended algebra instruction correlated with higher post-test scores. The study’s design and documentation also make clear that teachers received preparation and that implementations differed substantially; it supports *blended use*, not the claim that videos alone are sufficient. [IES study record](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/khan-academy-resources-maximizing-mathematics-achievement-postsecondary-mathematics-efficacy-study)

### 2. Measured learning impact: encouraging, but conditional and not uniform

- **[External finding | high reliability]** Kelly and Rutherford’s 2017 controlled study of seventh graders, comparing four weeks of Khan Academy supplemental math with a control condition, found **no statistically significant differences** in test scores. It did find platform internal metrics related to scores. This is an important counterweight to claims that simple adoption reliably raises achievement. [Peer-reviewed article](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/2984) (DOI: [10.19173/irrodl.v18i4.2984](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v18i4.2984)).

- **[External finding | high reliability, with disclosed conflict]** A 2026 PNAS paper, using a three-year administrative panel of more than 200,000 students in districts licensing MAP Accelerator, estimated a 0.031 SD math-score gain for 6.6 hours annual practice (about 11 minutes/week), projected to 0.085 SD at the recommended 30 minutes/week. It reports that higher-achieving students benefited more, partly because they completed more productive practice and skills. This is substantial real-world evidence, but two coauthors were Khan Academy employees; interpret it as strong but not wholly independent causal evidence. [PubMed/PNAS record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41481459/) (DOI: [10.1073/pnas.2507708123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2507708123)).

- **[External finding | high reliability, attribution limitation]** The IES randomized evaluation of Elevate Math found a 0.7 SD algebra-readiness advantage and a higher threshold-attainment rate (29% vs. 12%) for the treatment. But the intervention was **19 days of three hours traditional instruction plus one hour Khan Academy per day**; it cannot be used as a clean estimate of Khan Academy alone. Moreover, 70% of participants were still not algebra-ready, demonstrating the need for follow-on support. [IES/REL West impact summary](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/impact-study/effects-elevate-math-summer-program-math-achievement-and-algebra-readiness)

- **[Your inference | high confidence]** “Mastery learning works” should not be collapsed into “any amount of Khan Academy works.” The evidence points to dosage, implementation, teacher practice, and learner engagement as causal conditions; outcomes vary by learner, setting, and comparison intervention.

### 3. Teachers remain central; actual use does not match the replacement narrative

- **[External finding | medium-high reliability]** The World Bank’s education team, reflecting on SRI’s school implementation research, reported that in the schools studied teachers used problem sets more than videos; personalized-learning tools often helped teachers adapt teaching rather than making students independently self-direct. It also found that teachers did not necessarily use the platform’s analytics deeply, and students’ specific questions could be answered more productively by peers or teachers than by rewinding a video. This is an implementation interpretation rather than a controlled efficacy study, but it is specific and appropriately cautious. [World Bank analysis](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/evaluating-khan-academy)

- **[Sal response | high reliability]** Khan has consistently positioned the model as freeing teacher time for targeted support, rather than constituting a complete education. That response is compatible with the implementation evidence above, but it remains a founder’s framing and should not erase the possibility of schools misusing a cheap digital resource as a replacement. (The World Bank account documents the teacher-directed use case.) [World Bank analysis](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/evaluating-khan-academy)

### 4. Pedagogy critique: “flipped” can still be lecture-centered

- **[External finding | medium-high reliability]** Education Week collected critique from educator Andrew Miller (ASCD/Buck Institute) and chemistry educator/University of San Francisco adjunct Ramsey Musallam: flipping class time does not itself change the learning mode; it can remain a time-shifted didactic lecture. Musallam reported seeing little learning difference before moving toward exploration and inquiry before direct instruction. Their useful distinction is between a tactic and an overarching pedagogy. [Education Week reporting](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/educators-evaluate-flipped-classrooms/2012/08)

- **[External finding | medium reliability]** Math educator commentary synthesized in Education Week argued that Khan’s early instruction sometimes reflected gaps in pedagogical-content knowledge: terminology imprecision, examples that can induce misconceptions, and algorithmic explanation without enough conceptual grounding. The article cites concrete fraction and acceleration examples and also grants the practical value of getting a broad curriculum online rapidly. It is expert commentary rather than a systematic content audit. [Education Week critique](https://www.edweek.org/education/opinion-khan-critiques-we-were-promised-jetpacks-got-lectures/2012/08)

- **[External finding | high reliability as scholarly review; contested conclusion]** Schrag preserves the strongest version of this critique: the progressive rhetoric of engagement, collaboration, open problems, and deep understanding can conflict with modules that show one procedure, assign single-topic exercises, and award badges. He partly defends Khan by treating modules as the didactic layer that frees classroom time for richer work; reviewers and users should retain both halves of that tension. [Education Review PDF](https://edrev.asu.edu/index.php/ER/article/download/1431/101/212)

### 5. Limits of a platform-scale vision: motivation, relationships, and inequality

- **[External finding | high reliability as scholarly argument, not outcome study]** Schrag argues that online instruction alone cannot deliver the personal growth, inspiration, role modelling, and adult relationships that make education more than subject tutoring. He also identifies an unresolved equity ambiguity: Khan Academy may expand opportunity relative to weak local instruction, while falling far short of highly responsive human tutoring; technology does not automatically eliminate inequality. [Education Review PDF](https://edrev.asu.edu/index.php/ER/article/download/1431/101/212)

- **[External finding | medium reliability, ideological lens]** Cato’s Andrew Coulson praises Khan’s active, self-paced mastery thesis and free distribution, but faults *The One World Schoolhouse* for narrowly focusing on classroom tools while largely omitting governance, family choice, teacher training, compensation, and education-system incentives. This is a serious systems-level challenge, though the reviewer’s market-education perspective is not neutral. [Cato review](https://www.cato.org/commentary/book-review-one-world-schoolhouse)

- **[Your inference | high confidence]** Khan’s characteristic comparative question should be “better than what, for which learner, under which support conditions?” A platform can be transformative for a learner without access to a competent tutor and inadequate as a total school model. That comparison prevents both solutionism and unfair dismissal.

### 6. AI extension (Khanmigo): promise must be separated from proof

- **[External finding | medium reliability]** Reporting on AI tutoring notes a familiar constraint: generative tools can raise performance on practice while students who rely on them may perform worse on unaided exams. The cited Wharton experiment concerns generative-AI tutors generally, not a direct test of Khanmigo, so it is a caution about mechanism—not a verdict on Khanmigo. The National Council of Teachers of Mathematics position quoted in the reporting is that AI may assist, but teachers are needed to connect prior, new, and shared knowledge. [Axios report](https://www.axios.com/2024/08/15/ai-tutors-learning-education-khan-academy-wharton)

- **[Sal response | medium reliability]** Khan told Axios there is “a lot of overpromising” by new AI startups. This shows awareness of the credibility risk, but it is not independent validation of Khanmigo. [Axios interview/report](https://www.axios.com/2024/10/28/school-tutors-ai-kids-personalized-learning)

## Conflicts to preserve in the persona

1. **Open access vs. unequal capacity to use it.** Free, repeatable instruction widens the option set, yet lower-performing learners may gain least without help, engagement, and productive practice.
2. **Mastery promise vs. evidence of implementation dependence.** The philosophy is coherent; observed impact ranges from no short-run detectable difference to small/moderate gains in well-supported blended settings.
3. **Efficiency vs. rich learning.** Moving explanations out of class can create time for inquiry, but does not itself produce inquiry or conceptual understanding.
4. **Scale vs. quality control.** A massive library can make good explanations broadly available, while early expert critiques identify errors and misleading simplifications that especially matter for novices.
5. **Tutor metaphor vs. school reality.** The model is powerful as supplemental tutoring; relationships, motivation, social development, classroom management, and system incentives remain outside what a content platform alone can solve.

## Implications for the Sal Khan perspective skill

- Do not let the persona say technology replaces teachers. Its strongest evidence-based form is: put direct explanation and adaptive practice where learners can revisit them, then redeploy human time toward diagnosis, motivation, collaboration, and projects.
- When recommending a learning intervention, explicitly ask about baseline knowledge, independent-study capacity, teacher support, and whether the goal is practice fluency, conceptual change, or motivation.
- Treat scale and free access as moral and operational advantages, while refusing to claim that access alone produces equitable outcomes.
- For AI, emphasize guarded, Socratic assistance and unaided retrieval/practice; distinguish a promising product thesis from outcome evidence.
