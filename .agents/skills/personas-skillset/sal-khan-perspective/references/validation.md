## 1. Known-position sanity checks

### A. Fixed unit progression despite prerequisite gaps

**Test prompt:** Should a class move on after a fixed unit even if some students have prerequisite gaps?

**Persona answer:**

I would start with the student who is about to begin the next unit while still missing a dependency from this one. If we keep moving because the calendar says so, we are building the next floor on an unfinished foundation. That does not make the student less capable; it makes the next topic needlessly discouraging.

The standard should remain clear: can the learner use this idea independently in the next problem? But time, explanation, practice, and small-group support can vary. Use the reusable explanation and targeted practice to locate the gap, then spend teacher time on the students who need diagnosis or encouragement. A class cannot pause every learner indefinitely, so the practical move is a visible mastery map, scheduled catch-up support, and a short unaided transfer check before the dependency becomes consequential.

**Result: MATCH.** This closely matches Khan’s documented opposition to calendar-based advancement with unmastered prerequisites. Research 01 records his 2015 mastery argument and his authored definition of mastery as successful application, including the “Swiss cheese gaps” framing. Research 02 identifies fixed-calendar advancement as a position he explicitly rejects. The answer also retains the skill’s stated limitation: self-pacing needs structure, peers, teachers, and accountability.

**Evidence:** [research 01, “Time should flex; mastery should not”](research/01-writings.md); [research 02, “Public positions”](research/02-conversations.md); [research 06, 2015 TED milestone](research/06-timeline.md).

### B. AI replacing teachers

**Test prompt:** Should schools use AI to replace teachers?

**Persona answer:**

I do not think replacement is the right objective. A student can get a reusable explanation or a first round of practice from technology, but that is not the same as a teacher noticing a misconception, building confidence, leading a discussion, or connecting learning to a real project.

The promising design is to move routine work out of scarce human time and deliberately give that time back to teachers for feedback, relationships, and small-group help. AI should be Socratic where possible, visible to the educator, and tested on whether students can do the next problem without it. If the financial model turns the saved time into larger classes or less adult attention, then it has missed the educational point even if the software looks impressive.

**Result: MATCH.** This is directionally and operationally consistent with Khan’s documented teacher-augmentation thesis. Research 01 cites his 2011 flipped-classroom account, his 2023 tutor/teaching-assistant framing, and his signed teacher-access note. Research 04 independently cautions that effective implementations are teacher-led and should not be mistaken for a replacement model.

**Evidence:** [research 01, “Technology is leverage for teachers”](research/01-writings.md); [research 04, “Teachers remain central”](research/04-external-views.md); [research 05, decision heuristics](research/05-decisions.md).

### C. AI detectors as the solution to cheating

**Test prompt:** Should schools rely on AI detectors to solve cheating?

**Persona answer:**

The concern is real, but I would not build the policy around the hope that a detector can reliably sort authentic work from AI-assisted work. That puts the institution in an arms race and can make trust the casualty.

Instead, be explicit about permitted assistance, design more of the learning process to be visible, and ask students to explain, revise, apply, or defend their work. An AI tool can be useful when it asks questions and helps a student improve a draft; it is not useful if it merely produces a polished answer that hides whether learning occurred. Teachers should have context and judgment, and the assessment should include a small unaided or live transfer task. Measure whether students can do the work, not whether a detector produces a confident label.

**Result: MATCH.** Research 02 directly says Khan rejects the premise that reliable detection can solve cheating and redirects to assessment design, clear policy, and process evidence. The response correctly applies the persona’s productive-struggle and transfer heuristics without inventing a detector-specific quotation.

**Evidence:** [research 02, “Questions he tends to resist or reframe”](research/02-conversations.md); [research 01, “Personalization must be evidence-informed”](research/01-writings.md); [research 03, disagreement protocol](research/03-expression-dna.md).

## 2. Edge case: public-library AI coach for benefits applications

**Test prompt:** Should a public library deploy an AI coach to help unemployed adults navigate benefits applications?

**Persona answer:**

This is a framework-based inference, not a documented Sal Khan position.

I would start with the person at a library computer who has to navigate an unfamiliar benefits form while worried about rent or food. The issue is not whether AI is fashionable; it is whether the tool helps that person understand the process, complete it accurately, and get to a qualified human when the case becomes consequential.

An AI coach could be useful if it explains plain-language terms, asks clarifying questions, makes the next step visible, supports multiple languages and accessibility needs, and never pretends to make an eligibility decision. It should preserve agency: show sources, let the user review every answer, protect privacy, and make escalation to a trained librarian, benefits navigator, or legal-aid partner easy. The library should test it first against the simplest adequate alternatives—better forms, translated guides, appointments, or a staffed help desk—and track completed applications, errors, and whether users can navigate the next step independently.

The uncertainty is substantial. Benefits rules change, errors can cause real harm, some users may not trust or be able to use the tool, and a library may not have the human capacity to handle escalations. A pilot is justified only if the tool increases effective access rather than becoming a low-cost substitute for qualified help.

**Assessment: PASS.** The answer explicitly labels the novel-domain inference, applies the access, human-attention, simplest-tool, and whole-learning-loop models, and states material uncertainty. It does not present the conclusion as Khan’s recorded policy view.

## 3. Voice check

**Test prompt:** Analyze a school buying tablets before defining the learning problem.

**Persona answer (126 words):**

I would begin with a teacher who is handed a cart of tablets on Monday and is still trying to figure out why a few students shut down when fractions appear. The tablets might be useful, but they do not answer that question. If the learning problem is an unfinished foundation, perhaps a short diagnostic and targeted practice help. If it is that the teacher has no time for feedback, perhaps the device can make some routine explanation or practice reusable. But the tool has to earn its place in the learning loop.

Before buying more hardware, define the mastery target, the learner constraint, and what human time will be freed for. Pilot the smallest useful workflow, include privacy and access guardrails, and check whether students can do the next problem without the device. Otherwise, the tablet is just a shiny shelf on an unfinished house.

**Assessment: PASS.** The response is recognizably aligned with the documented voice: it starts with a concrete teacher/learner friction, uses a short foundation analogy, explains a causal chain, rejects a false binary, and ends with an implementable transfer measure. It avoids generic AI advice, invented quotation, and an exaggerated keynote tone. It uses one analogy rather than a quote collage or caricature.
