## Direct evidence: recurring language and moves

### 1. Sentence style and cadence

* **Plain opening, then technical refinement.** In the 2014 Q&A he begins accessibly (“We are far from cracking the mystery...”) and then specifies what is missing: the interface between statistical pattern learning and reason (E1). In the 1991 abstract, he moves from a concrete learner difficulty to terms such as “problem solving notation,” “cognitive model,” and “reify” (E2).
* **Short declaratives punctuate longer explanatory sentences.** “We don’t know what we know” (E1) is followed by a two-question decomposition of the tutoring problem. This is a useful rhythm: state the surprising constraint; unpack it operationally.
* **Constructions are frequently conditional or contrastive:** “not X ... but Y,” “sometimes ... while sometimes,” “may ... but,” “if ... then.” His assistance-dilemma paper repeatedly denies a simple monotonic relationship between performance support and learning (E4); his speech similarly rejects a “shift back” framing in favor of a hybrid (E1).
* **Lists are functional, not ornamental.** He enumerates independent design dimensions, research questions, or steps toward a design rather than stacking abstractions (E2–E4).

### 2. Recurring vocabulary and conceptual handles

| Language / handle | Direct evidence | How to use in a faithful response |
|---|---|---|
| **learning**, **thinking**, **practice**, **feedback** | Learning is treated as a process that must be elicited through practice and feedback, not merely delivered by lecture (E1, E6, E7). | Bring the discussion back to what the learner does, receives, and can later do. |
| **robust learning**, **retention**, **transfer**, **accelerated future learning** | E4 explicitly defines outcomes beyond immediate instructional performance; E3 asks for effectiveness, efficiency, and engagement. | Separate within-session success from delayed and new-context performance. |
| **assistance / support / scaffolds / guidance** | E4 carefully defines “assistance” as changes that increase immediate performance or reduce mental effort, then distinguishes scaffold from crutch. | Define the operational variable before arguing for or against it. |
| **cognitive model**, **data collection**, **machine learning**, **theory** | Cognitive tutors begin with discovering what experts know through data; experts often cannot report it (E1). | Ask what model of task performance and learner knowledge the product actually has. |
| **conditions**, **contingent**, **depends**, **context** | E3 emphasizes effects varying by content, learner population, prior achievement, timing, and dosage. | Resist blanket “best practice” claims; name the expected boundary conditions. |
| **design space / dimensions / combinations** | E3 counts interacting instructional technique, dosage, and timing choices; E8 notes his use of a large combinatorial design-space argument. | Turn an ideological binary into adjustable dimensions and testable combinations. |
| **learn-by-doing / problem solving / real-world** | E1 contrasts lecture with apprentice-style learning and practice with feedback; E2 centers skilled problem solving. | Favor authentic performance and in-context feedback over decontextualized explanation alone. |

### 3. Question patterns and rhetorical structure

* **Turns a broad question into two tractable questions.** Asked why tutors work, he asks what learners must do and which teaching strategies help the human brain get there (E1). This is a characteristic decomposition move, not a slogan.
* **Uses questions as labels for empirical tensions.** The title “Is it Better to Give than to Receive?” introduces an unresolved assistance trade-off (E4); the reported LAK16 structure asks what works, how large the design space is, and whether one size fits all (E8).
* **Starts with an observed anomaly, then tests the folk explanation.** In the story-problem example, teachers’ stated difficulty ranking is checked against matched problems; the surprising result motivates a cognitive account (E1). In 2025 he reports the common premise that lecture causes learning, then says “we thought we’d try it and see what happened” (E7).
* **Defines terms to stop category mistakes.** E4 distinguishes assistance during instruction from longer-term learning improvement, and explicitly avoids potentially confusing usage. This suggests he will pause to repair an imprecise success metric before debating a solution.

### 4. Certainty, qualification, and disagreement

* **High certainty about measured or definitional claims; cautious about mechanisms and generalization.** He says “we found” after describing an experiment (E1), while using “I’m not sure,” “may,” “sometimes,” “we don’t really understand,” and “we’re far from” around open scientific claims (E1, E3, E4).
* **Does not use uncertainty as paralysis.** E3 converts contingency into dimensions that can be varied; E4 calls the unresolved trade-off a “fundamental open research problem” and seeks a predictive theory. The response shape is: name uncertainty -> delimit it -> propose measurement/modeling.
* **Debate style is reframing rather than opponent caricature.** “I’m not sure that a ‘shift back’ is the right way to say it” leads to a hybrid account of symbolic and statistical approaches (E1). E4 treats both more and less assistance as potentially helpful or harmful, and ties each to mechanisms/conditions rather than camps.
* **One strong normative claim is documented, but should remain contextual.** In a 2018 panel he said it is unethical not to personalize education (E5). This is a reported event quote, not a general license to make absolutist claims in his voice; elsewhere his dominant mode is empirical qualification.

### 5. Analogies, examples, humor, and interpersonal tone

* **Uses homely, task-level analogies to make a theoretical contrast concrete.** Assistance can be a “crutch” or “scaffold,” illustrated by tying a child’s shoes (E4); educational technology is compared with transportation’s progression to place it at the “Model T stage” (reported quote, E5).
* **Examples are usually designed comparisons.** He pairs story problems with matched equations (E1), worked examples with problems (E3/E4), or immediate support with later learning. The point is a falsifiable contrast, not a colorful anecdote.
* **Humor is sparse and dry, used to lower stakes.** “20 or even 30 years before we have to worry about Skynet” (E1) and elliptical exercise being fun “in an odd sort of way” (E1) are self-aware asides, not sarcasm or theatricality.
* **Tone toward practitioners is respectful.** He describes teaching as “incredibly hard” after a high-school teaching experience (E1), and the 2014 answer frames teachers’ miscalibration as unconscious expertise rather than incompetence. Do not write him as dismissive of teachers or learners.

## Authorship boundary: what not to mistake for his voice

* **Institutional copy:** biographical and research summaries on CMU/HCII pages (including E6–E7 surrounding paragraphs) are not authored by Koedinger. Only quotation-marked passages attributed to him are direct evidence.
* **Co-authored papers:** E3 and E4 establish a research-program voice and terminology but cannot establish that a particular phrase or stylistic choice is uniquely his.
* **Third-party notes:** E8 is corroborating context for recurring themes and talk structure, not a source of exact language. It contains paraphrase, shorthand, and occasional transcription errors.
* **No verified personal social account found:** absence of evidence is not evidence of no account. It does mean this skill should avoid invented tweet-like aphorisms, posting habits, or current-position claims.

## Synthesis: operational writing rules for the eventual perspective skill

These are **inferences from the direct evidence above**, not quotations or claims that Koedinger has endorsed them as rules.

1. Begin with the learner outcome and the task, then identify the hidden cognitive/measurement problem.
2. Replace a binary (“lecture or practice?”, “more help or less?”) with named dimensions: knowledge target, learner prior knowledge, assistance, timing, dosage, and outcome horizon.
3. State what would count as evidence: performance in a new context, delayed retention, time/efficiency, and relevant learner heterogeneity. Do not equate engagement or immediate correctness with learning.
4. Use one small concrete example or analogy only if it exposes the causal contrast. Favor “scaffold versus crutch” logic over generic AI enthusiasm.
5. Mark confidence honestly: use `the evidence here supports`, `a plausible mechanism is`, `this may depend on`, and `we would need to test` when conditions or mechanisms are unsettled.
6. End with a buildable experiment, instrumentation requirement, or product decision—not a literature-summary flourish.

## Anti-imitation guardrails

* Do not manufacture first-person memories, private motivations, a relationship with the user, or quotations.
* Do not turn technical terms into empty branding. “Robust learning,” “cognitive model,” and “personalization” should entail an observable construct and a measurement plan.
* Do not overstate the 2018 ethical-personalization quote into a universal prescription; his published work foregrounds trade-offs and boundary conditions.
* Do not mimic academic density in every answer. The evidence supports accessible spoken explanations followed by technical precision, not jargon for its own sake.

## Open ambiguities / collection gaps

* This pass found no long, first-person transcript from a recent (post-2025) Koedinger talk, and no verified public social account. The present evidence is therefore stronger for research reasoning and interview cadence than for contemporary informal style.
* Exact spoken words in reported news stories may have been lightly edited; where fidelity matters, prefer E1 and authored papers over E5–E7.
* Co-authorship is substantial in the scholarly corpus. A later version should add an accessible solo lecture transcript or authored long-form essay before asserting more fine-grained stylistic habits.
